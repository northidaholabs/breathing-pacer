Breathing pacer: a guide for parents and teachers

A shape goes up, the child breathes in. It comes down, the child breathes out. A few breaths and it is finished.

How to use it

  • Do it with them. Sit alongside and breathe too. Children borrow calm from the adult next to them.
  • Practise when things are calm. A skill learned on a good morning is there on a hard afternoon. In the middle of a meltdown, most children cannot follow a pacer, and that is fine.
  • Keep it short. Five breaths is a full session. Two or three short goes across the day beat one long one.
  • Offer it. Do not require it. "Do you want the breathing shape?" works. "Go and do your breathing" turns it into a demand.
  • Let them stop. The stop button is always there. Stopping early is not failing.
  • Small, gentle breaths. Big gulps of air can make a child dizzy. Nobody needs to fill their lungs.

What it is not for

  • Not a consequence. If it follows trouble every time, it becomes a punishment and children stop choosing it.
  • Not something to earn or be rewarded for. There are no points or prizes in it on purpose.
  • Not a way to stop stimming. Rocking, flapping or humming while breathing is fine. Many children regulate that way.
  • Not a treatment. It is a calming activity. It does not diagnose or treat anything.

Setting it up for one child

PaceStart with the age button. If the child strains, sighs or gulps, make it faster. Comfort matters more than slowness.
SoundOff unless the child wants it. Listen to the tone or voice yourself first.
Shape and colourLet the child choose with you. Then leave it alone: the same every time is the point.
MovementIf moving shapes bother the child, choose the shape that stays still and fades.
WordsOff for children who do not read yet. "Breathe in" and "Breathe out" for those who like the label.

Making it a routine

Pick one fixed moment, such as after lunch or before the bus, and put the picture card on the child's visual schedule. Same place, same time, same shape.

When to stop and ask

  • Stop if the child feels dizzy, tingly or uncomfortable, or seems more upset by watching their breathing.
  • Some children find paying attention to their breath unpleasant. Believe them and try something else.
  • Ask the child's clinician first if they have asthma or a heart condition.
